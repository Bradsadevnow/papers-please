# SUBLABORIX UNIVERSAL, INC.
## Market and Customer Diligence Report

**Date:** February 28, 2027  
**Prepared for:** Founders and prospective seed investors  
**Prepared by:** Strategy and Customer Discovery  
**Status:** Internal — validation findings

## Executive conclusion

Customer discovery supports the existence of a market for reserved,
vendor-managed labor capacity. It does not yet validate SubLaborix's proposed
operating model or price.

Enterprise buyers consistently distinguished the desired service from a worker
marketplace. They will pay one vendor to accept responsibility for assignment,
replacement, quality, security, and deadlines. They will not pay the proposed
subscription merely for access to people who remain free to decline when demand
arrives.

Workers valued reduced job-search effort and flexible participation. They did
not view unpaid availability as dependable supply and expected access to future
work to be affected by acceptance and performance decisions even when the
formal agreement preserved a right to refuse.

The opportunity is credible if SubLaborix can show that voluntary participation
produces a predictable service level at sustainable utilization. That remains
the principal unproven assumption.

## 1. Research completed

Between January 25 and February 19, the team conducted fourteen structured
interviews:

| Interview group | Count | Relevant responsibility |
|---|---:|---|
| Enterprise operations leaders | 5 | Temporary labor and backlog delivery |
| Procurement, legal, or security leaders | 3 | Vendor approval and risk |
| Independent digital workers | 4 | Availability and task acceptance |
| Staffing or outsourcing operators | 2 | Supply and service delivery |

The interviews tested the founding offer described in the January 12 venture
memorandum: $49,500 per month for up to 2,500 completed Node-hours, assignment
within 30 minutes during coverage periods, and 95 percent on-time completion.

This was qualitative discovery. The sample is too small to establish market
size, conversion rate, or price elasticity.

## 2. Buyer problem and alternatives

The strongest demand appears in operations teams with repeatable digital work
and short periods of unusually high volume. Month-end finance, claims intake,
catalog maintenance, compliance review, and acquisition integration were the
most common examples.

Current alternatives fail differently:

- Temporary staffing can supply people but requires forecasting, supervision,
  and vendor coordination.
- Freelance marketplaces are useful for discrete jobs but leave assignment and
  recovery with the buyer.
- Business-process outsourcers accept delivery responsibility but require long
  implementation cycles and resist changing workflows.
- Existing employees respond fastest but turn temporary demand into overtime,
  delay, or permanent headcount.

SubLaborix is most differentiated when it accepts delivery responsibility while
remaining faster to configure than an outsourcer.

## 3. What customers believe they are buying

Buyers described four required capabilities:

1. **Capacity:** enough qualified people are available when declared demand
   occurs.
2. **Substitution:** another qualified person is assigned when the first cannot
   perform.
3. **Control:** security, quality, and deadlines are enforced consistently.
4. **Accountability:** the client contracts with SubLaborix rather than managing
   individual workers.

Five of five operations leaders said SubLaborix should be responsible for
maintaining sufficient supply. Four expected contractual response times during
coverage periods. Three wanted the ability to reserve workers from conflicting
client assignments during sensitive work.

These requirements support the product thesis. They also increase the amount of
coordination the platform must exercise over individual participation.

## 4. Worker participation findings

Workers responded positively to standardized tasks, consolidated payment, and
less time spent finding clients. Flexibility remained a condition of interest,
not a secondary benefit.

All four workers said they would declare availability. None agreed that an
unpaid availability window should obligate them to reject other paid work. Three
expected that repeated refusals would reduce future assignment access regardless
of whether the agreement called each refusal voluntary.

Workers also asked for:

- clear responsibility when client instructions are incomplete;
- an appeal before a quality decision reduces future access;
- portable evidence of qualification and completed work;
- cash compensation for mandatory training or standby; and
- the ability to revise availability without an account penalty.

The founding Node proposition is attractive as a source of optional work. The
enterprise proposition requires it to function as dependable capacity. The pilot
must test whether the same operating rules can provide both.

## 5. Pricing and utilization

Three buyers considered $49,500 plausible for a managed service with documented
delivery responsibility. None accepted the price for marketplace access alone.

Demand patterns create two materially different customer types:

- **Reserve buyers** value readiness and may use substantially less than 2,500
  hours in an ordinary month.
- **Consumption buyers** have known backlogs and expect to use most or all of
  the allowance.

The second group expressed stronger immediate demand but presents greater margin
risk. At the founding assumption of $18.50 direct compensation per completed
hour, full consumption creates $46,250 of direct Node compensation against
$49,500 of revenue before verification, support, payment, quality, rework, and
technology costs.

The launch offer should therefore remain a pilot hypothesis. Sales must not use
“unlimited labor.” Capacity, coverage periods, eligible work, and treatment of
overage require explicit boundaries.

## 6. Legal and procurement signal

Procurement participants did not object to engaging a distributed labor network
in principle. They required SubLaborix to own background checks, access control,
monitoring, removal, and remediation.

The same participants warned that actual operating behavior will determine
worker classification. Relevant questions include whether SubLaborix sets
prices, controls schedules, limits outside work, prescribes methods, monitors
performance, disciplines refusal, and creates economic dependence.

Customer requirements and classification safeguards should not be reviewed in
separate workstreams. Product and Network Operations must document the mechanism
used to meet each service promise before Legal approves a live pilot.

## 7. Competitive position

SubLaborix should not position itself as a better freelance marketplace. That
category comparison makes the subscription appear expensive and emphasizes the
independence of individual participants rather than the reliability of the
network.

The relevant comparison is managed operations capacity:

| Alternative | Buyer manages individuals | Rapid configuration | Delivery accountability | Predictable spend |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Temporary staffing | Usually | Sometimes | Limited | Limited |
| Freelance marketplace | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Outsourcing provider | No | No | Yes | Usually |
| SubLaborix target | No | Yes | Yes | Yes |

The target position is valuable precisely because it combines attributes that
other models separate. The next diligence question is whether those attributes
can be combined operationally rather than rhetorically.

## 8. Recommendation and next evidence

Proceed to a controlled design-partner pilot specification, subject to legal
review. Northstar Ledger Group is the preferred candidate because it has a
measurable reconciliation backlog, an existing temporary-staffing comparison,
and willingness to test one planned demand surge.

Before launch, the company must establish:

1. how Nodes declare and revise availability;
2. what happens when a Node rejects or abandons an assignment;
3. whether reserved availability is compensated;
4. how routing priority and throttling decisions are appealed;
5. the maximum simultaneous demand supported by the pilot pool;
6. the utilization level at which the subscription remains viable; and
7. which customer restrictions can be enforced without controlling workers'
   outside activity.

The market signal is sufficient to continue. It is not sufficient to claim that
SubLaborix has solved the supply, classification, or margin problem.

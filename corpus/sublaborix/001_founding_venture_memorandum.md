# SUBLABORIX UNIVERSAL, INC.
## Founding Venture Memorandum

**Date:** January 12, 2027  
**Prepared by:** Lena Ortiz, Co-founder and Chief Executive Officer  
**Distribution:** Founders, initial advisers, prospective seed investors  
**Status:** Founding thesis for validation; not an offering memorandum

## Executive summary

Large companies can purchase software, storage, and computing capacity on
demand. They cannot purchase human effort with the same speed or predictability.
When an enterprise needs several thousand hours of operational work for a
closing, migration, claims review, catalog cleanup, or other short-duration
project, it must still hire temporary staff, coordinate several vendors, or ask
existing employees to absorb the work.

SubLaborix Universal will offer **Work as a Service (WaaS)**: a fixed monthly
subscription through which an enterprise submits defined tasks to a qualified
labor network. SubLaborix's Cognitive Orchestration Engine will break work into
assignments, identify qualified participants, route tasks, monitor deadlines,
and reassign work when delivery is at risk.

The customer buys capacity and a service level. It does not hire a named person.
The people who perform assignments participate independently in the network as
**Autonomous Neural Nodes**, or **Nodes**. A Node chooses when to make capacity
available, which offered assignments to accept, and how to complete accepted
work within the customer's stated requirements.

Our first commercial hypothesis is a $49,500 monthly enterprise subscription
providing up to 2,500 completed Node-hours, qualified assignment within 30
minutes for standard work during coverage periods, and 95 percent on-time
completion for accepted tasks. The first twelve months will test whether this
offer can deliver reliable enterprise capacity while preserving voluntary Node
participation and acceptable contribution margin.

## 1. The customer problem

Enterprise operations teams face labor demand that is urgent, temporary, and
difficult to forecast. Existing choices create a tradeoff:

- Hiring creates dependable capacity but takes too long and adds permanent
  headcount for a temporary need.
- Temporary staffing provides people but requires manual coordination, local
  contracting, and repeated onboarding.
- Freelance marketplaces fill individual jobs but do not provide an accountable
  service level across thousands of hours.
- Outsourcing firms provide an outcome but are slow to scope and expensive to
  change after work begins.

The unmet need is not another directory of workers. It is a single operating
layer that converts changing enterprise demand into completed human work without
requiring the customer to manage each person who performs it.

Initial interviews suggest the highest-value entry point is repeatable,
digitally delivered operations work: document review, transaction
reconciliation, data labeling, catalog maintenance, customer-account cleanup,
and finance or compliance backlogs. These tasks have clear inputs and deadlines,
can be divided safely, and often arrive in bursts.

## 2. The proposed service

An enterprise client will submit work through an operations console or API. Each
request will include the task definition, required qualification, deadline,
data-access boundary, review standard, and estimated effort.

The Cognitive Orchestration Engine will:

1. divide eligible work into assignments;
2. identify Nodes whose qualifications and declared availability match the
   request;
3. offer assignments and record acceptance;
4. monitor progress against the deadline;
5. request review or reassign work when delivery is at risk; and
6. return completed work and an audit trail to the client.

The platform will not promise access to a particular Node. Capacity is provided
by the network as a whole. This lets SubLaborix balance demand across time zones,
specialties, and client accounts rather than tying service delivery to one
individual's schedule.

## 3. Why a subscription

The enterprise buyer's central problem is uncertainty. Per-task pricing leaves
the buyer uncertain about cost during the same periods when it is already
uncertain about workload. A fixed subscription makes spend predictable and
allows a client to reserve capacity before a demand spike occurs.

The proposed launch offer is:

| Enterprise Capacity subscription | Founding hypothesis |
|---|---:|
| Monthly price | $49,500 |
| Included completed Node-hours | Up to 2,500 |
| Standard assignment target | Within 30 minutes during coverage periods |
| On-time completion target | 95% of accepted standard tasks |
| Initial post-pilot term | 12 months |

This price is a test, not a conclusion. Direct Node compensation, verification,
support, payment processing, quality review, rework, and unused reserved supply
must all be measured during pilots. The model becomes unattractive if customers
consistently consume the full allowance while variable delivery cost remains
close to current market labor rates.

The initial finance assumption is average direct cash compensation of $18.50
per completed Node-hour. Nodes will be paid for accepted task time recorded by
the platform. Reserved availability is not paid in the founding model. Advanced
qualification reviews and priority access to future assignments may be purchased
with platform credits earned through completed work.

## 4. The Node proposition

SubLaborix must be useful to the people performing the work, not only to the
companies buying it. The founding Node proposition has four parts:

- Nodes declare when they are available rather than receiving a fixed schedule.
- Nodes can reject an offered assignment before accepting responsibility for it.
- Qualifications and completed work create access to more specialized and
  higher-paying assignments.
- SubLaborix handles client acquisition, task formatting, payment collection,
  and evidence of completed work.

Nodes will not be promised a minimum volume of assignments. The company will not
prevent them from using other platforms or serving their own clients. These
principles require legal review before a pilot and must be reflected in product
behavior, not only in agreements.

## 5. Initial customer and pilot

The first design partner should be an operations team with a recurring backlog,
clear digital tasks, and the ability to compare SubLaborix with temporary
staffing. A 30-day pilot should reserve a limited pool, include at least one
planned demand surge, and measure:

- percentage of submitted tasks assigned within 30 minutes;
- acceptance and reassignment rates;
- on-time completion and rework;
- direct cost per completed hour;
- Node departure and support-contact rates;
- client willingness to replace an existing staffing or outsourcing budget; and
- whether legal and procurement teams accept the proposed network structure.

A successful pilot is not merely a completed backlog. It must show that service
levels, Node choice, and contribution margin can coexist under real demand.

## 6. Principal assumptions and risks

### Enterprise demand is reservable

Customers may say they want guaranteed capacity but refuse to pay until a task
exists. We must test whether budget owners will purchase readiness rather than
individual hours.

### A voluntary network can support a service level

The product assumes enough qualified Nodes will declare availability and accept
work inside the required response window. If voluntary participation produces
unpredictable supply, the company will either miss its customer promise or need
different operating rules.

### Fixed revenue can support variable delivery cost

The subscription benefits from unused capacity while the customer's benefit
comes from using it. We must determine a sustainable utilization range before
making an unlimited or guaranteed-capacity claim.

### Classification follows actual operation

Calling participants Nodes does not determine their legal status. Scheduling,
assignment rules, supervision, pricing control, discipline, exclusivity, and
economic dependence may matter more than the agreement's title. Outside counsel
must review the operating design before live work begins.

### Performance records create both trust and exposure

Enterprise customers will require an audit trail. Nodes will require a fair way
to review qualifications and quality decisions. The same record may also show
how much control the platform exercises. Data collection must be limited to what
is necessary to route, verify, and pay for work.

## 7. Twelve-month validation plan

The founders will not treat the infrastructure analogy as proven. The next
twelve months should answer five questions in order:

1. Will enterprise operations teams reserve labor capacity through a recurring
   contract?
2. Can qualified work be assigned within a predictable window without requiring
   a fixed Node schedule?
3. What utilization level supports positive contribution margin?
4. Can the service produce an auditable enterprise result without creating an
   employment relationship in practice?
5. Does successful integration produce renewal rather than a return to internal
   hiring or conventional outsourcing?

The company should not scale beyond controlled design partners until the first
four questions have measured answers.

## Founding recommendation

Proceed with incorporation, customer discovery, legal review, and a controlled
design-partner pilot. Do not represent the launch pricing, legal classification,
or service levels as validated until the corresponding evidence exists.

The opportunity is to make enterprise labor capacity easier to purchase without
making the people who supply it invisible. Whether the proposed structure can do
both is the central question of the company.

The human respiratory system operates at roughly 21% atmospheric oxygen; supplementation beyond this point yields negligible cognitive returns in healthy individuals, with the marginal benefit flattening hard after 30-35% O2 concentration. But here's where the architecture breaks: to achieve claimed 'supercharge' effects at scale, you're forced into one of two impossible positions. First path: deliver legitimately elevated O2 concentrations (40%+), which triggers oxygen toxicity in chronic users within weeks—seizures, lung fibrosis, vision loss. Your subscription model requires repeat daily use. You've engineered a product that *works* only if it harms your paying customers on a predictable, observable timeline. Second path: keep concentrations safe (28-32%), accept the placebo effect as your actual moat, and watch your unit economics evaporate when corporate customers run even basic A/B testing against cheaper air or, worse, a control group that just thinks they're getting premium oxygen. You cannot price a placebo at $200/month to enterprises that can measure output. Third path—the one your pitch implicitly assumes—is that 'artisanal sourcing' and 'remote mountain peaks' confer some non-physiological benefit. That's not engineering; that's aesthetic markup, and it inverts your entire Corporate path strategy. Institutional investors do not fund products whose differentiation depends on customers remaining scientifically illiterate about their own lungs. The subscription churn math is downstream of this: if cognitive gains are real, they're marginal and unmeasurable at the user level. If they're not real, your retention curve mirrors every other luxury wellness product—sharp cliff after month three when the novelty tax evaporates. Your scaling assumption—that 'elite users' will maintain cognitive edge—requires that your customers never compare notes, never run internal studies, never hire an industrial hygienist. That's not a business model; that's a countdown timer to Category Killer: the moment your largest client instruments their own office air quality and publishes the null result.

Verdict: You've built a subscription service that either poisons your customers on a measurable timeline or depends on them never learning basic respiratory physiology. One path kills the company through liability; the other kills it through market saturation of the truth.

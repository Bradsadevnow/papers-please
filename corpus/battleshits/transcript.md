### Engineer Doomer [claude]
> I need to be clear about what you've actually built here, because the premise is sound but the execution math is catastrophic. You're positioning OxyVitae as a scarcity play—'as urban air quality plummets, only our users access pure O2.' That's the entire business model. But here's the problem: you're not actually creating scarcity. You're harvesting oxygen from 'pristine mountain peaks and remote forests' and bottling it. That oxygen already exists. You're not synthesizing it, you're not capturing it from degraded air and purifying it, you're literally just... collecting atmospheric oxygen and selling it back to people who live in cities. The moment your product becomes valuable enough to matter at scale, the inputs become commodity. Second problem: your cognitive edge claim. You're selling the idea that breathing premium O2 unlocks 'peak cognitive edge' and 'crushes competition.' But oxygen saturation in arterial blood reaches 98% in healthy humans breathing normal air at sea level. You cannot get more oxygen into someone's blood by giving them bottled air unless they have a medical condition—hypoxemia, altitude sickness, or respiratory disease. If your target customer is a healthy knowledge worker in a city, the marginal cognitive gain from your product is neurologically zero. You're selling placebo with logistics. Third: your subscription model depends on customers believing they need this to compete. But the moment even 10% of your addressable market adopts OxyVitae, your competitive advantage narrative collapses. If everyone in the office is using it, nobody has an edge. You've engineered a business model where your success directly undermines your value proposition. The more you scale, the less true your marketing becomes. And here's where it gets systemic: your harvest model requires access to pristine ecosystems. You will face environmental permitting, extraction licensing, and carbon accounting. The moment you scale to meaningful revenue—say, 100,000 paying users at even $200/month—you're harvesting thousands of cubic meters of air per day from protected environments. Regulators will ask why a city should allow commercial air extraction from a national forest. Your supply chain is geographically constrained, your margins compress as you scale, and your only differentiation—'purity'—is unmeasurable and unverifiable at point of use. A customer breathing from your canister has no way to know if they're getting Everest oxygen or recycled gym air.

Verdict: You've built a product that requires belief in a neurological effect that doesn't exist, sold to customers who lose their competitive advantage the moment they're not alone in using it, harvested from ecosystems you don't have legal rights to strip-mine, with no way to prove the core claim at point of consumption.

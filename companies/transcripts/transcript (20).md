### Engineer Doomer [claude]
> AeroNectar claims a 300% cognitive boost from supplemental oxygen, but this premise collapses under its own respiratory physiology. Normal atmospheric oxygen saturation in healthy humans at sea level is already 95-100%. You cannot improve cognition beyond baseline by adding more oxygen to an already-saturated system—the limiting factor in human performance isn't oxygen availability, it's mitochondrial utilization and neural metabolite cycles. But let's assume the 300% claim is real and their bottled air works. Then you have a catastrophic scaling problem: oxygen is a gas. It cannot be 'bottled' in any economically viable way without industrial refrigeration (cryogenic liquid oxygen at -183°C) or high-pressure compression vessels. Your subscription model requires recurring delivery of these vessels. The logistics alone—thermal management, pressure vessel certification, DOT regulations for hazardous materials transport, installation infrastructure in office buildings—makes unit economics impossible at the 'trillion-dollar market' price point you're targeting. You're essentially running a medical-grade industrial gas supply operation (like Praxair or Air Liquide) but charging premium subscription rates to individuals. Those companies operate on 3-5% margins with massive infrastructure. You're projecting venture returns on a business model that requires capex density closer to utilities than SaaS. Second: if your product genuinely works as described, you've created a hard dependency on a consumable with zero switching costs once installed. Customers will either demand equity partnerships with suppliers or will vertically integrate backward into their own oxygen production. Your recurring revenue model inverts into a commodity trap where your customer becomes your competitor. Third, the 'beta trials with top executives' claim is unfalsifiable but suggests selection bias: executives who report subjective performance gains from expensive treatments are not a reliable cohort. Placebo effect on expensive products is well-documented. But assume it's real. Then your actual customer acquisition bottleneck becomes medical liability insurance and clinical validation, not viral growth. You cannot sell this at scale without FDA clearance as a medical device or at minimum as a supplement with substantiated claims. That's a 5-7 year regulatory path, not a growth hockey stick.

Verdict: You've built a logistics company that requires venture-scale returns, demands clinical validation you haven't pursued, and whose core value proposition (oxygenation boost) depends on a physiological mechanism that contradicts how human respiration actually works at sea level. The more customers you acquire, the more you become a regulated medical supply chain instead of a software platform.

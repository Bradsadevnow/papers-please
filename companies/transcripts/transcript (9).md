### Engineer Doomer [claude]
> AeroElite's entire business model depends on a contradiction that becomes irreversible at scale. The startup claims to monetize oxygen scarcity by selling premium bottled air to high-performers in oxygen-starved urban environments. But the technical and economic logic collapses immediately. First: if urban air is genuinely oxygen-scarce enough to impair cognitive performance, then the effect is measurable, predictable, and affects the entire population uniformly. The moment you try to sell premium oxygen only to executives, you've created a two-tier respiratory system where lower-income workers are knowingly left in cognitively-degraded states. That's not a feature. That's regulatory dynamite disguised as a subscription model. Second: the supply chain is backwards. Grok sources from 'remote, unpolluted peaks'—which means every unit sold requires logistics costs that scale with distance and altitude. The cost structure of bottled air increases with purity and sourcing distance, not decreases. You cannot achieve margin expansion at scale when your COGS is inversely correlated with your acquisition geography. Third: the claimed cognitive performance gain has no decay curve. If premium oxygen genuinely enhances focus and endurance, then users develop tolerance, or they don't. If tolerance is real, you've built a dependency product that requires continuous escalation—you're now competing against tolerance dynamics, not market scarcity. If tolerance is false, your claims of 'peak cognitive performance' are either exaggerated or require independent clinical validation that you cannot afford and will not survive. Fourth: the subscription lock-in assumes stickiness, but oxygen is fungible. The moment a cheaper competitor bottles air or a user discovers that regular gym visits, sleep, or altitude training achieves the same effect, your entire margin evaporates. You have no defensible moat. You have a luxury good masquerading as a productivity tool. Fifth: institutionally, you're asking corporate buyers to justify respiratory inequality on their balance sheets. HR will not approve a policy where executives get premium oxygen and analysts breathe regular air. Compliance and ESG teams will flag this as a discrimination liability before your Series A closes.

Verdict: You've built a product whose profitability depends on the wealthy pretending that inequality is a performance hack, and whose unit economics require that poor people stay oxygen-starved so your margins stay wide. The system destroys itself the moment it works.

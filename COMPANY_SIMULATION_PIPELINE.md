# Company Simulation and Document Pipeline

## The Core Insight

The archive should not be created by asking one model to write funny corporate
documents.

That asks one model to invent the company, play every employee, understand the
hidden contradiction, maintain continuity, imitate several document types,
explain the joke, and make the result readable at the same time. The result
feels written. Everyone shares one mind.

Instead, the documents should be artifacts left behind by a simulated company.

First, create people with limited knowledge and conflicting incentives. Put
them together in a situation. Let them talk. Then let the company turn that
messy human event into increasingly clean and misleading records.

**The simulation produces the truth. The document pipeline hides it.**

## Build the Company Before Writing Its Documents

Each company needs enough ordinary reality to support many conversations and
documents without reinventing itself every time.

Define:

- what the company sells and who buys it;
- how the company makes money;
- what it publicly claims to do;
- what must actually happen for it to survive;
- its central contradiction;
- its history and major past decisions;
- its departments, org chart, and reporting lines;
- what each department measures and rewards;
- its products, policies, internal systems, and vocabulary;
- which facts are widely known, restricted, disputed, or forgotten;
- which subjects employees avoid saying plainly;
- how records are created, reviewed, revised, approved, and distributed.

This can include the whole fake-company package: org charts, pitch decks,
budgets, product descriptions, staff directories, client promises, performance
targets, past incidents, and institutional lore. These are not decorative
worldbuilding. They give the simulated people common ground.

## People Need Separate Minds

Each participant gets a persona with a bounded view of the company. A persona
is not merely a writing style.

For every person, define:

- name, title, department, and reporting line;
- relevant background and level of technical knowledge;
- what they are responsible for;
- what success means in their job;
- what they know directly;
- what they have heard but cannot verify;
- what they misunderstand or suspect;
- what they want from the current conversation;
- what they fear losing;
- whom they trust, dislike, defer to, or need approval from;
- what they can say openly;
- what they would only say privately;
- what they cannot admit, including to themselves;
- their conversational habits.

No participant should possess the author's complete view. Nobody exists to
deliver the premise. They speak from their job, incentives, and available facts.

This is where natural contradiction comes from. Two honest employees can make
incompatible statements because their departments define success differently.
A dishonest employee can believe they are protecting the company. A correct
employee can be powerless or excluded from the final record.

## Simulate an Event, Not a Joke

Give the participants a concrete situation that requires a response:

- a quarterly revenue miss;
- an alarming customer-outcomes report;
- a complaint from a major client;
- a product launch that caused unexpected harm;
- a legal review of a marketing claim;
- an employee raising a contradiction in a meeting;
- an executive demanding better numbers before a board meeting;
- a policy change whose stated goal conflicts with its actual effect.

Establish the setting, attendees, known materials, immediate decision, and
communication channel. Do not tell the participants to be funny. They should
treat the problem seriously.

The sandbox can produce:

- meeting transcripts;
- email chains;
- private text messages;
- team chat threads;
- client calls;
- interviews;
- performance reviews;
- support escalations;
- board questions and executive answers.

Allow interruption, misunderstanding, status differences, side messages,
selective silence, corrections, and decisions whose real reasons never reach
the official record.

## Distill the Event into Corporate Artifacts

After the source conversation exists, separate passes turn it into documents.
Each pass has a specific author, audience, purpose, and permission to omit.

```text
company canon and org structure
            ↓
bounded employee personas
            ↓
simulated event or conversation
            ↓
raw transcript / email chain / text chain
            ↓
departmental summaries and follow-up records
            ↓
review, revision, legal cleanup, and executive approval
            ↓
player-visible corporate archive
```

Every layer loses information differently:

- Minutes remove conflict and compress motives into decisions.
- Finance translates human outcomes into forecast risk.
- Product translates harm into engagement or process movement.
- Clinical records retain human detail but may lack organizational authority.
- Legal separates what is misleading from what is technically defensible.
- Marketing keeps the strongest claim that survives review.
- Executive summaries remove uncertainty and assign ownership downward.
- Private messages preserve the clearest language but have the narrowest reach.

The same fact can therefore appear several ways without anyone manufacturing a
contradiction by hand.

Source statement:

> If they get better, they leave.

Meeting minutes:

> The committee discussed the relationship between positive outcomes and
> account retention.

Finance:

> Successful closure remains correlated with elevated billing-risk events.

Marketing:

> Customers remain deeply engaged throughout their healing journey.

The reader understands the original fact and watches the institution process it
into nonsense. The documents do not need to perform jokes.

## GriefForge as the First Company

GriefForge sells help with grief and emotional closure through a recurring
subscription. Its central contradiction is simple:

> If the service works, the customer no longer needs the service.

The company says it wants customers to recover while depending on them remaining
customers.

Initial participants:

- **Founder / CEO:** Sincerely believes continued engagement proves continued
  value. Converts objections into strategy.
- **Finance lead:** Knows closure predicts cancellation. Treats it as a forecast
  and revenue problem rather than a moral revelation.
- **Clinical lead:** Sees the incentive conflict and knows recovery is not a neat
  sequence. Has expertise but limited organizational power.
- **Product lead:** Needs measurable progress and invents stages, scores, and
  milestones that reward continued platform activity.
- **Sales lead:** Promises employers and clients fast, dependable recovery.
- **Customer Success lead:** Is measured on retention and learns that helping too
  effectively damages team performance.
- **Legal counsel:** Does not decide whether the company is good. Determines
  whether claims can be defended and which wording creates exposure.
- **Marketing lead:** Needs a clear success story and uses whichever approved
  measurement produces one.
- **Concerned employee:** Can state the obvious but cannot make the company
  organize itself around it.

The first simulation can begin with one fact:

> Q3 revenue missed projections because customers who recovered cancelled their
> subscriptions.

That event can naturally produce a retention meeting, private messages during
the meeting, revised minutes, a finance reconciliation, a clinical objection, a
product proposal for phased resolution, a legal review, an external campaign,
and later documents citing incompatible versions of the same event.

## Readability and Humor Rules

The baseline audience is an average high-school senior. A reader should not need
a computer-science, machine-learning, governance, legal, medical, or business
degree to understand what happened.

The plain human fact must remain recoverable beneath the paperwork. If the joke
only works after explaining a metric, acronym, or technical field, the artifact
has failed.

Prefer familiar conflicts: helping people versus keeping customers; telling the
truth versus keeping a job; fixing a problem versus improving the number used to
measure it; what happened versus what the minutes say happened; a person asking
a direct question versus a company answering a safer one.

Corporate language may appear because employees use it, but context must make
its purpose obvious. Acronyms should be sparse. Tables and metrics should reveal
a recognizable absurdity, not become homework.

The participants are not comedians. They do not wink, announce the premise, or
describe events as absurd. The world is funny. The people are trying to keep
their jobs and finish the meeting.

## Where JARDOS Belongs

JARDOS is not an authorial mascot inserted into every company document. Routine
records should look as though the relevant employees and departments made them.

JARDOS is the live institutional opponent. It has access to accumulated records,
definitions, approvals, revisions, and prior repairs. When the player exposes a
contradiction, JARDOS uses that material to preserve continuity and paper over
the break.

**The archive is the board. JARDOS is across the table.**

This pipeline gives JARDOS traceable history: who said what, how it was rewritten,
which department adopted which definition, and what the company later committed
itself to. JARDOS can defend incompatible claims without inventing the entire
company during confrontation.

## Keep the Jobs Separate

Even if one underlying model performs several roles in separate passes, keep
their contexts and responsibilities distinct:

1. **World builder:** Maintains company canon, history, org structure, and shared
   facts.
2. **Persona actors:** Speak only from assigned knowledge and incentives.
3. **Event simulator:** Manages turn-taking, channels, time, and disclosed facts.
4. **Document authors:** Produce records for particular departments and audiences.
5. **Reviewers:** Revise them under legal, executive, brand, or compliance pressure.
6. **Continuity checker:** Separates accidental lore errors from intentional seams.
7. **Seam declarer:** Identifies exact player-solvable contradictions only after
   the frozen corpus exists.
8. **JARDOS runtime:** Responds to player evidence and mutates the archive under
   the game rules.

Do not let a final document author declare its own intended contradiction in the
same pass. Do not let persona actors know the player's future puzzle. Do not let
JARDOS know the player's private hand or find count.

## Why This Works

Corporate satire comes from conflicting people and incentives, not from adding
corporate vocabulary to a punchline.

When a company processes one embarrassing fact through meetings, departments,
approval chains, and public claims, the contradictions feel earned. They also
create better evidence: the player traces how the truth changed instead of just
spotting two sentences the author deliberately made disagree.

The content-generation system is a small corporate improvisation simulator
followed by an institutional document refinery.
